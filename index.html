How the score works

The score ranks signals by how well they did in a backtest of the S&P 500. Higher is better. It only means something compared with the other signals here; it isn't a probability or a return.

1. Test each 2-year period separately

History is split into seven periods: 2012–13, 2014–15, 2016–17, 2018–19, 2020–21, 2022–23 and 2024 to Nov 2026. Each signal is tested in each period on its own, and every measure below is the average of the seven, so a strong run in one market can't carry the score. A trade enters at the close of the day the signal fires, pays 0.05% in costs, and counts at most once per 5 trading days.

Signal rules use fixed levels, so nothing is fitted period by period. The exception: the zone levels for the three positioning signals (inverse ETF, vol-target, breadth) were chosen after looking at the full 2012–2026 history, so their results are less of an out-of-sample test.

2. Rescale each measure from 0 to 1

For each measure, the best signal gets 1, the worst gets 0, and the rest fall in between. That puts win rates, Sharpe ratios and signal counts on the same scale.

3. Weight and add them up
